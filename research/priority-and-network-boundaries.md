## Findings and limits

### AUTODIN II design work explicitly separated user, TCP, and backbone addressing roles

**Assessment.** Directly established in both 1976 TCP and THP Appendix A. BBN also recommended logical addresses invariant under changes of attachment; these are different levels of evidence from demonstrated mobility.

**Evidence.** SRI TCP and THP, July 15, 1976, pp. A1-A3; BBN 3119, TM-24, pp. 158-164.

### AUTODIN II priority requirements supplied an argument for removing sequencing from the backbone

**Assessment.** Directly established as a recommendation in Kahn's draft review; its implementation and influence on later IP require separate evidence.

**Evidence.** Kahn draft review, p. 11, especially the final paragraph.

### The August 1975 review was a DCA-convened committee, not Kahn's report alone, and Cerf and Postel served on it

**Assessment.** Directly established.

**Evidence.** Kahn draft review, Executive Summary, first numbered page; Appendix A.

### The recommendation to remove backbone sequencing was argued from interconnection and end-to-end encryption as well as priority

**Assessment.** Directly established.

**Evidence.** Kahn draft review, printed p. 12, paragraphs b and c; p. 10, recommendation 22; p. 11.

### The delivered AUTODIN II access protocol assigned resequencing to hosts or a higher-level protocol, naming precedence among its causes

**Assessment.** Directly established in the specification in force in March 1979.

**Evidence.** SIP TN-78-07.3, 5 March 1979, printed p. 1-4 / PDF p. 11; executive summary, printed p. i / PDF p. 5.

### DCA decided to adopt the committee's sequencing recommendation

**Assessment.** Not established. The recommendation and the later specification agree, but no decision record between them is held, and independent arrival from ARPANET experience remains open.

**Evidence.** The November 1975 System Performance Specification, the June 1978 Final Design Plan, and SIP TN-78-07.2 of 3 October 1978 are each named by held documents and none is recovered. The public sweep of September 17, 2026 identified SIP TN-78-07.2 as NTIS **AD-A064936**, Kulkarni and Sevcik, 3 October 1978, 70 pages, and did not locate the other two through the searched routes; see [DCA requirements and SIP identity](priority-and-network-boundaries.md#dca-requirements-sip-identity-and-the-classified-appendix).

### The AUTODIN II backbone carried a higher-level protocol identifier it did not interpret

**Assessment.** Directly established for the March 1979 specification; its introduction date is not established.

**Evidence.** SIP TN-78-07.3, printed p. 1-7 / PDF p. 14 and printed p. 2-11 / PDF p. 31.

### Postel's 1977 split proposal was motivated by AUTODIN II requirements

**Assessment.** Not established by the proposal: IEN 2 does not mention AUTODIN, precedence, priority, or security, and argues from layering and an ARPANET analogy. Its silence does not disprove an influence. Shared personnel and a shared problem cluster remain.

**Evidence.** IEN 2, August 15, 1977, full-text search and printed p. 1; Kahn draft review, Appendix A and printed p. 12.

### AUTODIN II work caused the TCP/IP split

**Assessment.** Plausible influence through specification experience and modularity arguments, not established causation or exclusive origin. INWG proposals and the TCP meeting record document a wider design setting and competing concerns.

**Evidence.** 1976 TCP and THP appendices; BBN 3119; Kahn draft review; INWG 96, pp. 3, 5, 11; INWG 96.1, sections 1, 4-5; IEN 2; IEN 66, pp. 6-7.

### The committee review: proposed responsibilities

G. Falk and J. McQuillan, *AUTODIN II Study Contract: Final Report*, BBN Report 3119, September 15, 1975, covering November 7, 1974 through August 31, 1975.

[Report documentation page](../sources/unique/bbn-3119/bbn-3119-capture-01.jpg), [recommendations, pp. 2-3](../sources/unique/bbn-3119/bbn-3119-capture-02.jpg), [recommendations, pp. 4-5](../sources/unique/bbn-3119/bbn-3119-capture-03.jpg), [message-header discussion, pp. 172-173](../sources/unique/bbn-3119/bbn-3119-p172-173.jpg). These pages were checked visually.

This DCA study recommends explicitly recognizing a host-to-host protocol, separating several interacting protocol functions, and using distributed server hosts for some services. Page 172 argues that the communications subnetwork should not assume a particular host protocol or format. These are recommendations within AUTODIN II development, and they show why a simple opposition between wholly inflexible AUTODIN II engineering and wholly general Internet research would be inaccurate. The concrete version, interface, and procurement conflicts are more informative. The report was searched through OCR and selected passages inspected; its full set of technical memoranda has not been assessed individually.

**The report supplies its own date and membership, recovered in the Chunk 1 pass.** The [Executive Summary](../sources/unique/committee-review-1975/committee-review-1975-pExecutive-Summary-first-numbered-page.jpg), the first numbered page, was inspected in the original. It states that DCA requested an ad hoc technical advisory committee of computer-network experts, assembled at short notice, whose members participated individually and not as representatives of their employers, and that the committee did not consider whether DCA should proceed with AUTODIN II but only whether the specification would produce the desired product. The committee met on **26 and 27 August 1975 at the Reston facility of the DCEC**, opened with a briefing on the RFP and technical specification by Dr. Lebow, Colonel Stathopoules, and Dr. Helm, presented its findings orally on the afternoon of 27 August, and briefed General Paschall the following morning. The summary says the report's findings "were unanimously agreed upon by the committee," which extends the unanimity already noted for the release recommendation on p. 2. The meeting date is now established from the document rather than from the working manuscript; a **final** report date still is not.

**Appendix A names the committee** (the OCR heading "APPENDIX 4" is a misreading; the [original photograph](../sources/unique/committee-review-1975/committee-review-1975-pAppendix-A.jpg) reads "APPENDIX A"): R. E. Kahn (ARPA, chairman), V. G. Cerf (Stanford University), M. Pirtle (NASA/Ames), J. Postel (Stanford Research Institute), J. Markowitz (Central Intelligence Agency), D. Wood (Mitre Corporation), A. Vezza (Massachusetts Institute of Technology). This matters for attribution in two directions. It settles that the recommendations are a committee's rather than Kahn's alone, and it places **Cerf and Postel inside the body that recommended moving sequencing out of the backbone**, two years before Postel's split memorandum. It is a documented shared setting, not a statement that the later argument derived from this one; see the Chunk 1 findings below for the control evidence against that inference.

The photographs remain a **draft recommendation**; they do not establish which recommendations DCA adopted. Their substantive importance is already clear: internetting ideas were being used to argue for changes to AUTODIN II. A one-way account in which its procurement requirements simply dictated TCP/IP would miss this interaction.

The March 1979 [AUTODIN II SIP specification](https://archive.org/download/DTIC_ADA071670/DTIC_ADA071670.pdf), executive summary, printed p. i / PDF p. 5, was also visually checked. It describes extensive redesign and packet transport without connection/sequencing functions at the SIP level. This is compatible with the draft's direction, but the two documents alone do not establish a causal chain or the behavior of every layer in AUTODIN II. **Correction from the Chunk 1 pass: SIP is the "Segment Interface Protocol," not the "Subscriber Interface Protocol."** Its full identity, read from the report documentation page, PDF p. 4, is *Initial AUTODIN II Segment Interface Protocol (SIP) Specification, TN 78-07.3*, report number DCA 200-C-637-P003, author V. R. Kulkarni, Western Union Telegraph Company, Government Systems Division, McLean, Virginia, controlling office DCA Code 450, dated **5 March 1979**, 68 pages. The abstract states that it "revises and replaces the 3 October 1978 version, identified as TN-78-07.2." That superseded October 1978 edition has not been recovered.

### Architectural questions

This section answers the architectural question at the level of protocol responsibilities and operating assumptions. A deeper influence need not survive as an unchanged field: requirements can make designers distinguish functions, assign responsibility, or discover that two services cannot share one mechanism. The evidence supports examining AUTODIN II as a setting for such work. It does not yet warrant assigning the fundamental Internet architecture as a whole to that program.

### Priority requirements and the backbone boundary

Additional pages of Kahn's draft *Comments on the AUTODIN II Phase I Technical Specification and a Set of Recommendations* were checked visually in this follow-up:

- [Page 4](../sources/unique/committee-review-1975/committee-review-1975-p4.jpg), recommendations 3-4, separates DCA's responsibility for the overall subscriber service from the contractor's operation, maintenance, and development of the packet-switching backbone. It explicitly connects this management division to a technical separation between backbone switching and subscriber access equipment.
- [Page 7](../sources/unique/committee-review-1975/committee-review-1975-p7.jpg), recommendations 10-13, proposes independent evolution of host protocols and the backbone, DCA validation of implementations, a reference implementation, and separately contracted front-end equipment. Certification of equipment outside DCA's direct control is one of its stated reasons.
- [Page 11](../sources/unique/committee-review-1975/committee-review-1975-p11.jpg) argues that moving sequencing to the host or access equipment would confine deadlocks and permit changes to the host protocol without changes to the backbone. Its final paragraph makes a particularly direct connection: required subnet priority mechanisms can demand delivery out of sequence.

Read those pages with the already cited [page 10](../sources/unique/committee-review-1975/committee-review-1975-p10.jpg), which links the redesign to robustness, interconnection, and possible end-to-end encryption, and [page 13](../sources/unique/committee-review-1975/committee-review-1975-p13.jpg), which recommends gateway interconnection and independently changeable headers. Pages 10 and 13 were also rechecked.

**The architectural mechanism is explicit:** precedence, sequencing, failure containment, and independent protocol evolution could pull in different directions. The review used AUTODIN II's required services to argue for redistributing functions across the system. This is stronger evidence for a fundamental influence than simply observing a military label in a header. It also gives the institutional argument a concrete form: who was responsible for providing and certifying a service was connected to where its functions should reside.

The March 1979 SIP specification's executive summary describes redesigned packet transport without connection and sequencing functions at that level. That later design is consistent with the recommendations. The present record has not established the decision chain linking them. Nor does a recommendation to remove sequencing from this backbone establish that AUTODIN II originated IP's datagram service. The source itself invokes ARPANET experience. A useful claim is that AUTODIN II supplied a consequential occasion to formulate and apply this functional separation.

### The recommendation and delivered sequencing endpoints

This subsection records the Chunk 1 pass of September 11, 2026. Its question was whether AUTODIN II's priority requirements led to a documented reassignment of sequencing between the backbone and hosts or access equipment, and separately whether any source connects that work to the research TCP/IP split.

#### Committee pages and stated reasoning

**Additional committee pages inspected in the original.** Five photographs beyond those already cited were read directly.

- [Executive Summary](../sources/unique/committee-review-1975/committee-review-1975-pExecutive-Summary-first-numbered-page.jpg) and [p. 2](../sources/unique/committee-review-1975/committee-review-1975-p2.jpg): the meeting dates, the committee's individual-capacity participation, its five determinations, and the unanimous recommendation that the RFP with the current technical specification not be publicly released without major revisions.
- [Page 3](../sources/unique/committee-review-1975/committee-review-1975-p3.jpg): the five areas of comment (management strategy, modularity and flexibility, technical problems, security, system performance and user issues) and recommendation 1, that the deliverable be specified in terms of performance criteria. Its illustrative quotation asks for hosts connected "to a packet switch using a standard communications protocol which allows, but does not require the host to be co-located with the switch."
- [Page 5](../sources/unique/committee-review-1975/committee-review-1975-p5.jpg): recommendations 5 and 6. Backbone equipment should be under the contractor's operational control if the contractor is held responsible for backbone service, on the stated ground that backbone performance can be well specified while the overall system's cannot; DCA should have operational control of the subscriber access equipment, the TAC and host access equipment. The exact wording of that contrast clause sits across a skewed line break in the photograph and is paraphrased here rather than quoted. Recommendation 6 limits the contractor's transit-time and throughput responsibility to the backbone, because "it is inappropriate to require the contractor's performance evaluation to depend on the performance of Host protocol implementations and of customer provided equipment."
- [Page 6](../sources/unique/committee-review-1975/committee-review-1975-p6.jpg): recommendations 7 to 9, including "loose coupling" between modules with uniform, cleanly defined interfaces, the logical and physical modularity of the hosts, the security subsystem, and the backbone, and an instruction to avoid a monolithic non-decomposable switch architecture. It also corrects a transit-time figure carried from OCR: the committee calls a specified 1 second transit time "excessive and unnecessary" and asks for **0.1 second** for an average 500-bit packet, adding that restricting the contractor's performance responsibility to the backbone "will make it possible for him to meet transit time and throughput requirements independent of the performance of Host systems."
- [Page 12](../sources/unique/committee-review-1975/committee-review-1975-p12.jpg) was reread in full. Paragraph b is the pass's most useful new passage and had previously been summarized only as a remark on packet sizes and time-outs.

**The committee's own stated reason ties sequencing to internetworking.** Page 12, paragraph b: "Provision for interconnection will also be facilitated if sequencing is performed at the Host level. Fragmentation to accommodate different maximum packet sizes, end-to-end encryption and the like is far easier to implement and will perform better if sequencing is based on Host level protocols." It then asks the RFP to give guidelines for source-to-destination flow control "which is independent of sequencing" and to propose a system plan "compatible with allowing computers on different packet switching networks to communicate assuming they have implemented the standard host protocols. The nets will in general have different packet sizes, time-outs etc." Paragraph c adds that the host/packet-switch interface "should be compatible with end-to-end security and with gateway interconnections to other networks. Another network should be treated as a particular type of Host connection." The argument therefore runs from three requirements at once, priority, end-to-end encryption, and interconnection with differently parameterized networks, to one conclusion about where sequencing belongs.

#### Technical agreement and the dated chain

**The recommended boundary and the delivered specification agree, at both ends of the chain.** Recommendation 22, printed p. 10: "the committee strongly recommends that the requirement for sequencing be eliminated from the backbone packet switching network. In lieu of this, sequencing shall be provided by a front-end computer, the users' mainframe computer or by a Host access mechanism." Page 11 supplies the mechanism, "move the sequencing function out of the backbone and into the front-end mainframe or TAC involved," and closes with the priority argument: "priority mechanisms which are required within the subnet, demand that certain messages be delivered out of sequence."

The March 1979 SIP implements that allocation and gives the same reason. Printed p. 1-4 / PDF p. 11, verified in the original image: "Adaptive routing and retransmissions within the backbone might cause out-of-order arrivals at the destination SIP. Also, since segments of differing precedence from multiple sources can arrive at the destination simultaneously, the SCM-to-SIP segment stream will be multiplexed and unordered. Further, under abnormal conditions (i.e. switch failure) the backbone may lose or duplicate segments, therefore, no guarantees against possible duplication, loss, or nonsequenced delivery to the destination SIP are made. Recovering lost segments, resequencing of out-of-order arrivals, demultiplexing segments from multiple sources and end-to-end accountability are the responsibilities of the end users or any higher level protocol acting in behalf of the end users." The executive summary states the same thing positively: the access components (TAC, SCCU, MCCU) and directly attached hosts get "pure packet transport service," and "the protocol is not burdened with connection and sequencing functions."

Two further SIP provisions belong to the same boundary and are new to this file.

- **A protocol identifier the backbone ignores.** The Binary Segment Leader carries a "TCP Version Number" field: "This field identifies the originating SIP's user (TCP, other high level protocol, utility, etc.). It is ignored by the network and is used by the destination SIP to pass the segment on to the proper higher level protocol" (printed p. 1-7 / PDF p. 14, inspected in the original). Printed p. 2-11 / PDF p. 31 assigns the values: "The code for the AUTODIN II TCP is one. The code for no TCP is zero. All other codes are reserved and will be assigned by DCA Code 530." This is the concrete form of the committee's recommendation 10, that the backbone be insensitive to changes in the host protocol, and of BBN 3119 TM-29's argument that the subnet should not assume a particular host protocol. It is also the mechanism that made a later host-protocol substitution possible without touching the backbone, which bears on Chunk 2. Do not read it as an AUTODIN II invention: TCP versions were already numbered in the research work, and this pass did not establish when the field entered the SIP.
- **Certification at the specified boundary.** Printed p. 1-11: "The remainder of this document only specifies the stimuli/response functions of the host SIP interpreter. These are the functions which will be tested and certified before a subscriber host is permitted to operate on the network. The internal SIP interfaces are purely host unique and are therefore not specified." That is recommendations 11 and 12 in operation, and it is the point where the technical boundary and the certification boundary are the same line.

#### Adoption and attribution limits

**Conclusion 1, change within AUTODIN II: the reassignment is established at both ends and the decision between them is not.** An August 1975 committee unanimously recommended removing the backbone sequencing requirement and named the three places the function should go, arguing in part from the priority requirement. The specification in force in March 1979 assigns exactly those responsibilities and names precedence among its reasons. What is missing is any record of the decision itself. The November 1975 System Performance Specification, the June 1978 Final Design Plan, and the October 1978 SIP are each named by documents in hand and none is held; those three are where adoption, partial adoption, or independent arrival would be visible. Independent arrival remains live: the committee's own p. 11 argues from ARPANET logical-link experience, and BBN 3119 was recommending related separations before the review.

**Conclusion 2, influence on the research TCP/IP split: strengthened as setting, contradicted as stated motive.** The new evidence for influence is the committee roster, which puts Cerf and Postel in the room, and p. 12's explicit chain from host-level sequencing to fragmentation across networks with different packet sizes, end-to-end encryption, and gateway interconnection. That is the later split's cluster of concerns, argued in August 1975 about a procurement.

The control evidence cuts the other way and is recorded here rather than left implicit. [IEN 2](https://history.rfc-editor.org/ien/ien2.txt), August 15, 1977, was searched in full text for AUTODIN, precedence, priority, and security. **None of those terms appears.** Postel argues from the principle of layering and from an explicit ARPANET analogy, host-to-host protocol above a hop-by-hop protocol with an 1822-like interface between them. Its proposed hop-by-hop protocol carries a Version field, whose stated purpose, "simultaneously supporting two (or more) versions of the protocol," matches what the SIP's TCP Version Number field does at a different layer, but IEN 2 attributes nothing to AUTODIN II. [IEN 66](https://www.rfc-editor.org/ien/ien66.pdf), p. 5, likewise discusses type-of-service indicators and where they should sit without naming the program. So the pass leaves the split question where it found it, with a better-documented shared setting and still no source in which a participant derives the split from the AUTODIN II work. A published claim should say that Cerf and Postel served on the committee that made the recommendation, and stop there.

### The specification’s identity and a second-hand page

This subsection records the Chunk 1 continuation of September 16, 2026. Its one bounded action was to recover the November 1975 *System Performance Specification*, the document the September 11 pass named as the place adoption would show. The specification was not recovered through the routes searched. Four things were established instead, and two adjacent leads the earlier pass had left open were closed.

**The interface protocol originates in the missing specification, and its 1979 text may not be read back onto it.** The March 1979 SIP's executive summary, printed p. i / PDF p. 5, read in [the original image](https://archive.org/download/DTIC_ADA071670/DTIC_ADA071670.pdf#page=5): "This is the initial Segment Interface Protocol (SIP) Specification. It is the formal protocol specification developed as a result of extensive redesign and associated changes since **it was first defined in the original System Performance Specification**." The record already used this page for its positive description of packet transport service; the sentence about origin was not extracted from it.

This cuts both ways, and both halves matter. The host/backbone interface was a defined protocol in the document DCA issued three months after the August 1975 committee review, which places the boundary's institution early and in the right document. But the same sentence says the protocol was extensively redesigned between then and 1979, so the sequencing language quoted at [sequencing endpoints](priority-and-network-boundaries.md#the-recommendation-and-delivered-sequencing-endpoints) from printed p. 1-4 cannot be assumed to have stood in November 1975. The gap in the chain is narrower and better described; it is not closed.

**The specification is a type A specification, and its amendment state is now known.** Three independent witnesses give the designation: the SIP's own background list at printed p. 1-2 / PDF p. 9, read in [the original](https://archive.org/download/DTIC_ADA071670/DTIC_ADA071670.pdf#page=9); reference 1 of Cerf's SCCU/MCCU note; and reference 19 of Whittenton's thesis, read in [the original](https://archive.org/download/DTIC_ADA080484/DTIC_ADA080484.pdf#page=184). The record's "type WAN" was an OCR artifact and is corrected above.

A fourth document fixes the amendment state. Western Union's *Computer Program Development Specification — Terminal Access Controller, Appendix B, Access Line Modes*, DCA 200-C-637-P007, April 1979, is the same contract and the same P-series as the SIP, which is P003. This is the priority requirement at the head of the chunk's chain, given a measurable shape for the first time, from the document that has never been held. It does, however, bear on adoption, cautiously. That is far from the one second the committee criticized and in the neighbourhood of what it asked for. The figures are not strictly comparable, since packet size, hop count and the mean-versus-maximum distinction all differ, and independent arrival remains live. #### Criticality and priority requirements

**The criticality structure behind the priority requirement.** Section 4.2.1.3.2.1 at printed p. 10 / PDF p. 13, read in [the original](https://s3.us-west-1.wasabisys.com/chm-cms-media/media-live/_file/bulkupload/102697716-05-0001.pdf#page=13), maps Flash Override (Y and W) and Flash (Z) to **Category I**, Immediate (O) to II, Priority (P) to III, and Routine (R) to IV. Appendix A explicitly identifies several layers of addressing and aims to conceal their differences from higher-level protocols so users see a coherent model. Its distinctions and mappings are concrete:

| Role | Representation and mapping in Appendix A |
| --- | --- |
| User address | Subscriber ID, User ID, and Function Suffix identify a terminal user, a host user/process, or a host function. |
| TCP address | A socket combines a 16-bit TCP identifier with a 16-bit port. For a host, Subscriber ID becomes the TCP identifier; User ID and Function Suffix map into the port. |
| Backbone destination | The Binary Segment Leader carries the subscriber address. In the host case this identifies the host's access circuit and cannot by itself identify a user or function. |

This establishes applied architectural separation within AUTODIN II. The host's TCP identifier is equated with Subscriber ID, and the BSL destination denotes subscriber access rather than the individual process. **A correction to the earlier interpretation:** denoting an access destination does not make the address value an immutable physical port number. BBN 3119 explicitly recommends logical-to-physical mappings and fixed subscriber addresses under changes of attachment, discussed below. The shared appendix specifies roles and mappings; by itself it establishes neither deployed mobility nor connection-preserving multihoming.

[IEN 2, *Comments on Internet Protocol and TCP*](https://history.rfc-editor.org/ien/ien2.txt), August 15, 1977, p. 1, was also checked visually. Postel argues for separating host conversation control from internetwork packaging and routing, with an explicit interface between them. He draws an ARPANET analogy. This supplies a later architectural position by an author of the AUTODIN II work. Neither inspected document says the later position was derived from that work. The same person, earlier applied distinctions, and later modularity argument make a plausible route of influence; they do not complete the causal proof.

### BBN 3119: alternatives and allocation of functions

G. Falk and J. McQuillan, *Final Report for the AUTODIN II Study Contract*, September 15, 1975, covering November 7, 1974 through August 31, 1975. The [photograph folder](../sources/README.md#reading-and-retrieval-records) contains the technical memoranda supporting the summary previously inspected. Existing OCR of its 155 photographs was reused for discovery. The following decisive original pages were inspected in this continuation. Dates here are the final report's date and coverage, not newly established individual dates for every incorporated memo.

| Architectural issue | Recommendation or argument in the report | Evidential consequence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Host error control | [Pp. 32-33](../sources/unique/bbn-3119/bbn-3119-p32-33.jpg): provide host detection/retransmission of lost messages and rejection of duplicates, explicitly drawing on failures experienced with the ARPANET host protocol. But p. 32 judges subscriber-switch and switch-switch error detection sufficient to make a host checksum unnecessary. | Strong evidence for deciding responsibilities through AUTODIN design work; also evidence against treating its recommendations as identical to the eventual end-to-end TCP design. It selectively trusts the network. |
| Switch-independent host protocols | TM-29, [p. 172](../sources/unique/bbn-3119/bbn-3119-p172-173.jpg): the subnet should not assume a particular host protocol or format; subscribers might use the standard, a special protocol, or no explicit host protocol. The example includes host error and flow control. | Explicit architectural separation and freedom for host protocol evolution. It does not itself identify the later IP layer or explain the TCP/IP split. |
| Small switch responsibilities and distributed services | [P. 74](../sources/unique/bbn-3119/bbn-3119-p74-75.jpg): reliability and cost favor minimizing switch functions; distributed server hosts should provide special services and permit future extensions. Logical addressing and some NCC functions are candidates for distribution, while essential single-focus control remains at the NCC. | This allocates functions among switches, service hosts, and control authorities. It is more specific than equating every form of distribution with moving all state to end hosts. |
| Address semantics and alternatives | TM-24, [pp. 158-159](../sources/unique/bbn-3119/bbn-3119-p158-159.jpg): distinguishes physical switch/port numbers from fixed logical subscriber addresses translated by the network. It criticizes the February 1975 specification's hybrid scheme because ordinary physical-address subscribers still face problems when moved. [Pp. 162-163](../sources/unique/bbn-3119/bbn-3119-p162-163.jpg) compare adaptive distributed mapping, server-based mapping, and host routing. [P. 164](../sources/unique/bbn-3119/bbn-3119-p164-165.jpg) recommends fixed logical addresses invariant under changes of access termination, preferring partitioned mapping. | A documented consideration of alternatives, not just resemblance to a later concept. It directly qualifies the earlier inference from THP/TCP's phrase "host's access circuit": access destination and physical address encoding must be distinguished. Formal adoption of this mapping scheme is not established by the recommendation. |
| Multihoming and failure | TM-31, [pp. 182-183](../sources/unique/bbn-3119/bbn-3119-p182-183.jpg) and [p. 184](../sources/unique/bbn-3119/bbn-3119-p184-185.jpg): prefer assigning an entire transaction to one access circuit over distributing individual segments, avoiding additional host reordering machinery while using multiple attachments. [P. 178](../sources/unique/bbn-3119/bbn-3119-p178-179.jpg) still requires host recovery from losses/duplicates after failures and proposes a special switch-state transfer mechanism explicitly absent from the current design. | This explores survivability and host/network work allocation. The transactions, logical channels, and proposed resynchronization are not measurements of TCP connection continuity. The preferred design retains significant switch state; it cannot be used as straightforward proof of a stateless IP network's origin. |

These recommendations broaden the case beyond security/precedence fields: AUTODIN II was a concrete setting for arguments over error control, protocol independence, address translation, service placement, and failure recovery. They also show competing allocations and continued reliance on subnetwork machinery. The evidence supports **documented architectural reasoning within the project** and, in BBN 3900, **documented adaptation to the emerging TCP/IP architecture**. Neither establishes that AUTODIN II caused the initial host/network division, selected the final Internet address model, or caused Postel's split proposal. Those causal claims need correspondence or decision records linking the work.

### Administrative agreement in the shared service

The feature analysis above has an architectural implication: the protocols could make an administratively assigned classification or precedence relevant to whether a connection was permissible and whether it remained valid. That requires participating components to interpret those attributes compatibly, and hosts to mediate applications' authority to request them. This is a more substantial operating assumption than the mere availability of optional metadata. The later conflict documented in RFC 2873 demonstrates the consequences of treating an administrative service value as an invariant of a transport connection while intermediate networks acquire reasons to change it.

Keep the claim specific. [RFC 675, section 2.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc675.html#section-2.2), December 1974, already describes local ownership of ports and checks on which processes may use connections. AUTODIN II cannot be credited from this evidence with introducing all host authorization. The trace concerns security, precedence, and TCC authorization in particular. Modern MLS labeling provides a specialized continuation; ordinary TCP does not require a DCA-style global subscriber directory. Packet labels also do not prove that the original system had one uniform, centrally administered implementation of every control.

### Architectural attributions the evidence leaves open

| Candidate | Evidence and assessment |
| --- | --- |
| Endpoint reliability and unsequenced packet carriage | RFC 675, pp. 1-4, already combines gateway interconnection with endpoint retransmission, ordering, flow control, and socket-pair connections. Kahn's AUTODIN II review gives additional reasons to move sequencing out of the backbone. Influence through reinforcement or application is plausible; the 1976 AUTODIN II branch is not established as the origin of these mechanisms. |
| Survivability and keeping transport state at endpoints | [Clark's 1988 design-philosophy paper](https://groups.csail.mit.edu/ana/Publications/PubPDFs/The%20design%20philosophy%20of%20the%20DARPA%20internet%20protocols.pdf), pp. 107-108, connects military priorities, surviving network/gateway failures, and endpoint state. This is participant testimony about the wider DARPA Internet. It does not identify AUTODIN II as the specific cause. |
| TCP connection identity tied to network addresses and ports | Both companion appendices give a concrete AUTODIN II mapping to subscriber/access addresses, while BBN 3119 proposes stable logical addresses. RFC 675 already embeds network and TCP identifiers in sockets. A claim that AUTODIN II selected the binding that later limited mobility needs evidence of the alternatives considered and the selection decision. The existence of a compatible mapping alone is insufficient. |
| A common internetwork protocol across heterogeneous facilities | Kahn's recommendations and the 1978 upgrade order demonstrate consequential work on this problem within AUTODIN II. The final IP architecture retained local network formats and address mappings; it did not require every underlying switch or network to abandon its own protocols. A claim that gateways ceased encapsulating packets conflicts with [RFC 791, section 2.2](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc791.html#section-2.2), which explicitly describes that encapsulation. Preserve the distinction between a common IP layer in hosts/gateways and the networks carrying its packets. |
| A smaller set of routing and administrative relationships | The planned AUTODIN II long-haul role is relevant context. IEN 30 already recognizes separate organizations, and the DDN backbone must be distinguished from the larger Internet. A direct AUTODIN II cause for global addressing choices, restricted routing policy, or later autonomous systems remains unproven. |

Clark also connects the priority of survivability over resource accounting to the military setting, and discusses difficulties turning architectural flexibility into procurement performance requirements (pp. 107, 110-111). These are useful broader questions about what standards left to implementations and purchasers. They need AUTODIN II-specific contracts or decision records before being used as evidence of that program's individual influence.

### Missing decision evidence

No recovered correspondence or decision record connects the 1976 AUTODIN II work to the 1977–78 TCP/IP split. Current source comparisons do not establish that causal connection.

The SRI specification itself supplies a firmer citation link to the wider work. Cerf's resynchronization note is a specific follow-up source, not newly examined content.

For competing explanations of the split, Postel's [IEN 66 meeting notes](https://www.rfc-editor.org/ien/ien66.pdf), meeting October 13-14, 1977, notes dated October 21, **printed/PDF pp. 6-7**, were inspected. They discuss moving fragmentation/reassembly into a separate internet protocol, judge that too large a change for TCP-3, and consider it for TCP-4. The next page discusses speech, conferencing, low delay, and possible unreliable service. This is direct evidence of the contemporary design agenda and incremental decision process. These passages do not attribute those changes to AUTODIN II. Read them with IEN 2's modularity argument, rather than replacing their stated concerns with an inferred single cause.
