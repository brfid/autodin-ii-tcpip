# The BLACKER security interface: reserved for separate work

[Research overview](../research.md) · [Open questions](../questions.md) · [Sources](../sources/README.md)

This material remains outside this research. The March 1985 next-hop allowance predates BBN’s March 1986 criticism, but the separate reported keying restriction, guide identity, operational usability, and AUTODIN II ancestry remain unresolved.

Read the [findings and limits](blacker-interface.md#findings-and-limits), the [supporting evidence](blacker-interface.md#evidence), or the [unresolved questions](blacker-interface.md#unresolved-questions). Recorded reading boundaries are in the [reading guide](../sources/coverage.md).

## Findings and limits

### DDN's authorization resolved all conflicts between defense requirements and Internet architecture

**Assessment.** Not supported; a specific security-interface incompatibility was still being criticized in 1986.

**Evidence.** BBN 6223, p. 19 and footnote 2. Its direct derivation from an AUTODIN II requirement is not established.

### The 1989 BLACKER ICD introduced a gateway-address remedy to the 1986 criticism

**Assessment.** Contradicted for this clause: the 6 March 1985 BFE ICD already permits the distinct next-hop destination. Whether BBN correctly described a separate keying restriction remains unresolved because its cited guide is missing. The addressing clause alone does not establish fully usable gateway support.

**Evidence.** *DDN Protocol Handbook*, Volume One, December 1985, AD-A166324, printed pp. 1-34 to 1-35; local netinfo `blacker.doc`, March 21, 1989, section 2.4.3, pp. 2-10 to 2-11.

### BBN’s 1986 security-interface criticism

BBN Report 6223, *The DoD Internet Architecture*, **draft dated March 20, 1986**, prepared for DCA under contract DCA200-85-C-0023, Task Order 5-32. The title and all pages cited here were inspected visually.

- [Title and date](../sources/unique/bbn-6223/bbn-6223-capture-01.jpg)
- [The larger internetwork, pp. 2-3](../sources/unique/bbn-6223/bbn-6223-capture-02.jpg)
- [Protocol implementation issues, pp. 16-17](../sources/unique/bbn-6223/bbn-6223-capture-03.jpg)
- [Host addressing and security-interface conflict, pp. 18-19](../sources/unique/bbn-6223/bbn-6223-p18-19.jpg)
- [Conclusion, pp. 24-25](../sources/unique/bbn-6223/bbn-6223-capture-05.jpg)

Page 3 describes an operational Internet including MILNET, ARPANET, and other networks, jointly run by DCA and DARPA. A footnote excludes the other DDN segments, which remained physically separate for security reasons. The conclusion describes local military networks connecting to the DDN long-haul network through IP gateways while retaining autonomy. This supports the distinction about the closer match between DDN and the developing Internet architecture.

Pages 16-18 report reluctance to implement TCP/IP in end-user equipment, arising from gaps in vendor support and the costs of changing functioning systems. The report argues that protocol translation short of the end hosts gives up benefits of the Internet's reliability, addressing, and routing architecture. It recommends a migration path toward host TCP/IP and individually addressable hosts behind LAN gateways.

**Page 19 supplies an especially concrete tension.** The report calls the draft DDN security architecture incompatible with that common-user interface. The passage was read from the page image and reads: "The 'DDN Subscriber Guide to Security Services, 1985-1992' specifies that a KI-111 (an end-to-end encryption device) will be placed on the access line between a classified LAN gateway and the classified segment of the DDN. This is logically the correct place for the KI-111. Unfortunately, the KI-111 interface is oriented towards a single host and does not support connection to a common user system. (2) This is a serious shortcoming in the DDN security architecture that should be remedied." Footnote 2 reads: "In particular it establishes connection keys based on the X.25 source and destination addresses and requires that these match their counterparts in the IP header. See p. 57." The year range falls inside BBN's quotation marks, so it belongs to the title as BBN gives it.

This is a contemporaneous engineering critique of a specific draft interface, not a demonstration that all DDN traffic suffered the problem. The recovered BFE ICDs specify a next-hop destination allowance that predates this criticism; **9d** compares it with BBN's report of a keying and address-checking rule in a different document. The guide itself is needed to resolve that apparent tension. Its direct lineage from a particular AUTODIN II requirement remains to be established. It does show that moving toward a shared Internet architecture left conflicts over how defense security requirements should be implemented. The successor system belongs inside the argument rather than serving merely as a chronological bridge to NSFNET.

For a public contemporary cross-check, the National Research Council's [February 1985 report reproduced as RFC 942](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc942.html), pp. 12 and 33-35, describes cancellation in favor of ARPANET-derived designs, DDN component networks, and operational MILNET gateways. It also records security-related limits on interconnection. This is an advisory report, not itself a DDN policy order.

### BLACKER editions, keying rules, and missing decision sources

The *BLACKER BFE ICD*, **6 March 1985**, already permits a next-hop gateway address distinct from the final IP destination. It is reproduced as section 4.2.2 of the [*DDN Protocol Handbook*, Volume One, *DoD Military Standard Protocols*, December 1985](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA166324), prepared by SRI International's DDN Network Information Center for the DDN Program Management Office; NIC 50004/50005/50006, DTIC AD-A166324, 610 PDF pages. The ICD occupies printed handbook pages **1-25 to 1-40**, PDF pages **34–49**, with its own internal pagination as well.

**Section 2.4.3** starts at printed handbook **1-34** (ICD p. 10, PDF p. 43), describing the host's use of the Red IP destination to determine the next hop on the Red Virtual Network. It continues at **1-35** (ICD p. 11, PDF p. 44): "The local subnetwork address will be the X.25 address corresponding to either the actual Red IP destination or the IP address of the gateway that is the next hop to the destination." Section **1.1**, printed **1-25** / PDF **34**, defines services on the "host (host or gateway) or plaintext side," and its diagram labels the attachment "HOST OR GATEWAY." All three pages were rechecked visually against an independently recovered, identically fingerprinted PDF.

**The destination provision predates BBN's March 20, 1986 draft.** The March 21, 1989 edition restates the permission and adds the example of IP devices not directly connected to a BFE. It therefore did not introduce this allowance as a response to that draft. March 6, 1985 is the earliest recovered edition date, not a documented date of introduction; no change record or stated rationale for the clause is held.

**BBN reports a separate keying and address-checking rule.** Its footnote concerns both source and destination X.25 addresses and their counterparts in the IP header. Section 2.4.3 directly establishes a destination-forwarding allowance; it does not settle source-address checks, key authorization, or the requirements in the guide BBN cites. No equivalent matching rule was found in the recovered ICD text readings. The 1989 section **2.3.18** defines a logical channel by a source X.25 address, a destination X.25 address and precedence; that definition is absent from the shorter 1985 clause, but it is not itself a cryptographic keying rule. The evidence leaves an apparent tension between documents. It establishes neither that BBN misdescribed its source nor that the specified destination allowance supplied a fully usable common-user interface.

The local [*BLACKER Interface Control Document*, March 21, 1989](https://mirror.cyberbits.eu/textfiles.com/internet/blacker.txt) is native ASCII text with internal page breaks, despite its `.doc` extension. Its archive wrapper says `netinfo/blacker.doc` and `8/89 AM`. A [Textfiles mirror](https://mirror.cyberbits.eu/textfiles.com/internet/blacker.txt) reproduces the body with wrapper, line-ending and trailing-blank differences; a [TUCoPS copy](https://artofhacking.com/tucops3/hack/networks/live/aoh_blacker.htm) supplied a discovery transcription. These are copies of one document, not independent testimony or a revision approval history. Appendix B reference 6 gives April 1989 as the final date for a Western Digital chip manual, but sections **2.2** and **3.2** explicitly call it an **expected** final publication date. This does not establish editing after the March 21 title date.

Both BFE ICD editions explicitly adapt the December 1983 *DDN X.25 Host Interface Specification* (AD-A137427; also local `netinfo/x25.doc`). Section 1.4 of the 1985 ICD identifies that dependency and limits X.25 to a local interface. The comparison needs to keep address conversion, IP forwarding, architectural device placement and cryptographic authorization distinct:

| Document | IP and X.25 addressing | Gateway provision | Security and authorization provisions |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

The conversion alone does not determine whether the selected address is a final destination or a next hop. | A-3.2 extracts IP datagrams at the local X.25 entity for onward transmission; A-4 allows cross-gateway communication with IP and Standard service, while excluding Basic-only hosts. | No cryptographic keying rule found in the checked text. |
| **DDN Subscriber Security Guide**, MITRE for DCA, November 1983 (AD-A152524) | No BLACKER address-matching rule specified. | Section 6.1 proposes BLACKER on all hosts with manual gates, or only on classified hosts with multilevel secure gateways. These are placement alternatives, not the single-host interface restriction BBN reported. | BLACKER automatic key distribution is anticipated; IPLI and gate security receive fuller treatment. This is a related earlier guide, not an established earlier edition of BBN's source. |
| **BLACKER BFE ICD**, March 6, 1985 | 2.4.3 distinguishes the datagram's Red IP destination from a next-hop gateway's X.25 address. | 1.1 explicitly permits host or gateway attachment. | 2.4.5 describes four current hierarchical levels and four National Access Programs, with extensions to eight levels and sixteen categories; 2.4.6 guarantees buffering at least five datagrams awaiting ACC authorization. |
| **DDN Subscriber Guide to Security Services, 1985-1992**, cited by BBN | Text unrecovered; BBN reports X.25/IP source and destination matching for connection keys. | Per BBN, KI-111 sits between a classified LAN gateway and DDN, but cannot support the intended common-user interface. | The guide's p. 57 and its relationship to the ICD remain unchecked. |

The changed wording of section 2.4.5 does **not** demonstrate a doubling of design capacity: the larger range was already described as an extension in 1985. The editions distinguish current labels, designed capacity and an intended initial configuration; they do not establish which labels an operating installation supported. The 1983 guide's alternatives are checked at printed **23** / PDF **33**; the X.25 comparison uses native text and the visually checked derivation at printed **A-9** / PDF **35**, with the worked result continuing onto **A-10** / PDF **36** as a text reading.

#### Availability and operational evidence

**Availability evidence concerns specific programs and dates.** Three sources describe expected availability or report BLACKER unavailable for the use under discussion. [DDN News 39](https://ftp.ripe.net/rfc/museum/ddn-news/ddn-news.n39.2), **April 26, 1985**, seven weeks after the ICD's edition date, makes DODIIS integration conditional: the subnetwork "will be integrated into the DDN Integrated Secure Network (DISNET) when blacker devices become available." The December 1985 handbook prints the approved architecture at printed **1-48** / PDF **57**: "All subscribers on the classified segment are connected to the DDN via the Internet Private Line Interface (IPLI), or equivalent end-to-end encryption (E3) devices," while BLACKER appears in the future tense as a development programme that "should provide remote key distribution and a trusted (multilevel secure) E3 device." An Army memorandum written between mid-December 1989 and **February 5, 1990**, reproduced in [DoD IG report 90-097](https://web.archive.org/web/20251207235621/https://media.defense.gov/1990/Jul/05/2001714385/-1/-1/1/90-097.pdf) at printed **61** / PDF **70**, then gives as its operating reason for retaining dedicated circuits that "the BLACKER encryption device is not yet available and DDN Milnet cannot process classified data at this time." The handbook and IG pages were checked visually; the newsletter is native ASCII. These sources distinguish the specification from reported availability and the published architecture. They do not establish a continuous, system-wide deployment history between 1985 and 1990. No checked source shows section 2.4.3 exercised in service; absence of that evidence is not proof that it was never exercised.

[Cisco's IOS command reference](https://www.cisco.com/c/en/us/td/docs/ios-xml/ios/wan/command/wan-cr-book/wan-x2.html) documents an `x25 remote-red … remote-black …` table for emergency-mode address translation. This corresponds to section 2.3.17.3 in both ICDs and to the black-side translation described in 1985 section 3.5.3 / 1989 section 3.4.3. The command is evidence of documented vendor support for that related feature, not a test of the next-hop allowance. No checked source establishes operational use of section 2.4.3. Appendix A's intended DSNET I configuration is not such a record. [RFC 4949, pp. 37–38](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4949.html) retrospectively identifies the BFE as TSEC/KI-111; [RFC 980, Appendix A](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc980.txt), March 1986, independently lists the BFE ICD in the 1985 handbook.

#### The missing guide and its citations

**The guide BBN cites is absent from the checked DDN catalogues, and a draft successor is documented in the right window.** Dated NIC and DDN Program Management Office catalogues were checked across BBN's stated range and past the end of it. [DDN News 37](https://ftp.ripe.net/rfc/museum/ddn-news/ddn-news.n37.1), December 1, 1984, records the November 1983 *Defense Data Network Subscriber Security Guide* "being entered into the DTIC," which dates its AD-A152524 accession. Six editions of NIC 50002, the *DDN Protocol Implementations and Vendors Guide*, were recovered from the Internet Archive DTIC mirror. Five of them carry a publications section, and each names only the November 1983 guide: [August 1986](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA173060) (AD-A173060), [August 1987](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA187689) (AD-A187689), [February 1988](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA192186) (AD-A192186), [August 1988](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA199688) (AD-A199688) and [February 1989](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA206354) (AD-A206354). The sixth, [February 1986](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA165418) (AD-A165418), is titled *TCP/IP Implementations and Vendors Guide*, is organised by vendor and has no publications section at all, so its silence is structural rather than evidential. The [August 1990 edition](../sources/unique/netinfo/vendors-guide.txt) lists no successor either. [netinfo/nic-pubs.txt](../sources/unique/netinfo/nic-pubs.txt), revised December 1991, again names only the November 1983 guide, and the bibliography of the *DDN New User Guide*, [NIC 60001, February 1993](../sources/unique/netinfo/nug.txt), still gives "DDN Subscriber Security Guide ... 1983 [AD-A152 524]" and nothing after it. That last witness postdates BBN's stated range. No successor is named in the sampled catalogues; the samples do not establish that no successor circulated. The NIC's own file-and-publication list, [netinfo/what-the-nic-does.txt](../sources/unique/netinfo/what-the-nic-does.txt), separately lists the security guide among NIC publications with no online pathname, while `netinfo/vendors-guide.doc` and `netinfo/x25.doc` are given one. It was a hardcopy publication. A full-text search of the 610-page December 1985 handbook finds no occurrence of "Subscriber Guide," "Security Services," "Subscriber Security" or "Security Guide" anywhere in the volume. Against that, [DDN News 43](https://ftp.ripe.net/rfc/museum/ddn-news/ddn-news.n43.1), January 15, 1986, reporting the first DDN Communications Operations Group meeting of **August 27-28, 1985**, records that "several draft documents were on display" and "will be available in final form in the near future," among them "the DDN Security Guide." A draft security guide therefore existed at the start of BBN's range and was not yet final. This is consistent with BBN having cited a draft-stage successor not listed in the catalogues checked, which would explain both the year range and the non-recovery. It remains a hypothesis: no recovered document bears BBN's title, the draft's contents are unknown, and p. 57 is still unread. Nothing here shows BBN misdescribed its source.

**The guide is attested outside the NIC's catalogues, with an author and two dates.** The September 16 full-text search of the Internet Archive found four DCA contractor reports that cite it, read from the DTIC mirror. The earliest is SPARTA's [*Requirements for the Next Generation Packet Switch*](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA167803), **22 April 1986** (AD-A167803). Its reference list at printed **59** / PDF **67**, checked visually, gives "Shirey, R.W., Defense Data Network Subscriber Guide to Security Services, 1986-1992, Draft, DCA, Code B600, **18 January 1986**," directly beneath Shirey's 1983 *Defense Data Network Subscriber Security Guide* (MITRE MTR83-W00137, dated there August 1983). The OCR layer misreads the date as 1966. Computer Sciences Corporation's [*Employment Concept for IDS Reconstitution*](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA195904), **31 May 1988** (AD-A195904), gives "30 September 1986, Mitre Report" at printed **1-5** / PDF **19**, checked visually. SPARTA's Trusted Guard Gateway reports of [10 May 1988](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA196891) (AD-A196891, printed **4** / PDF **8**) and [2 February 1989](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA210248) (AD-A210248, printed **5** / PDF **10**) footnote the same title as a DCA document; those two were read as text. So a draft by the author of the 1983 guide existed two months before BBN's March 20, 1986 draft cited it, a dated version followed in September 1986, and DCA contractors were still citing it in 1989, yet no checked NIC catalogue lists it. That strengthens the draft-stage reading above. It does not show which text BBN used, what p. 57 required, or that the September 1986 version was final. The title variant is unreconciled: BBN reads "1985-1992," and all four witnesses read "1986-1992."

CSC's report also summarizes the guide at printed **2-11** / PDF **33**, checked visually. The summary lists the guide's security services: confidentiality, split into mandatory, discretionary and traffic-flow; integrity; availability; and identification, authentication and access control, with data-origin authentication and non-repudiation. It assigns traffic-flow confidentiality on dedicated lines to KG-84A link encryption, and mandatory and discretionary confidentiality, support for integrity, and identification, authentication and access control to end-to-end encryption (E3). That is a contractor's summary of services, not the guide's text. It names no KI-111, no BLACKER and no address or keying rule, so it neither confirms nor contradicts BBN's reading of p. 57.

The same reports identify **CHM 85-1188** as a companion rather than a candidate. SPARTA's references at printed **57** / PDF **65**, checked visually, give "Defense Data Network Evolution of Security Services: 1986-1992, DDN PMO, DCA Code B610, Draft of 6 August 85 (with corrections, 30 Aug 85)," which matches the CHM entry's title, year range and draft date. CSC dates the same title "24 November 1985, Mitre Report" (printed **1-4** / PDF **18**, checked visually). All four reports cite the Evolution document and the Subscriber Guide as separate references. The Evolution draft is three weeks older than the DDNCOG meeting at which a draft "DDN Security Guide" was on display; which of the two drafts was shown there is not established.

#### NIC catalogue editions and retrieval handles

**The "update pending" note is now dated, and it does not point where it appeared to.** The September 14 pass traced the sentence through the recovered NIC 50002 series. It is absent from the August 1986 and August 1987 editions, first appears in **February 1988**, and is carried in August 1988 and February 1989; the August 1990 edition, whose own text records "Last edit: April, 1990," does not contain the word "pending" at all. Two things follow, and they pull in opposite directions. The note appeared three editions earlier than the record previously had it, which extends the period in which a replacement was being announced. But in every edition that carries it, the same sentence sits on the **DDN Subscriber Interface Guide** of July 1983 as well as on the November 1983 security guide, and both sentences disappear together without a successor for either. A pending update announced for both subscriber guides at once, carried for three editions and then dropped, could reflect a general revision effort, but neither its scope nor its abandonment is documented. It is therefore weaker evidence of a security successor in preparation than the February 1989 sighting alone suggested. The separate bounded finding is that no successor is named in the catalogues checked; circulation outside those lists remains unknown. No edition carries a change note for its publications section — the "REVISIONS THIS EDITION" section introduced in August 1987 records only new and discontinued products — so none of them states why the sentence was added or removed. The August 1987, February 1988 and February 1989 pages were read as images; the series and its fingerprints are in the [Chunk 5c record](../sources/records/nic-publication-editions.json) and its [edition extract](../sources/extracts/nic50002-editions-1986-1993.txt).

**The ICD is listed as a NIC online publication by August 1986.** The same NIC 50002 series lists the BFE ICD as a file available by FTP or Kermit. The August 1986 edition, at printed **5** / PDF **20**, checked visually, reads: "The Blacker Front End Interface Control Document contains the specification for end-to-end data encryption on the DDN. It may be obtained via FTP or Kermit from the SRI-NIC.ARPA host using pathname NETINFO:BLACKER.DOC and is also reproduced in the 1985 DDN Protocol Handbook." Every later edition checked keeps the entry, dropping the year; by August 1990 the host name has changed from SRI-NIC.ARPA to NIC.DDN.MIL and the pathname has not. So the document carrying section 2.4.3 was not only printed in a three-volume handbook: the DDN Program Management Office's own catalogue offered it as a retrievable file, from at least August 1986. This bears on availability, not on rationale, and it has a limit worth keeping: the catalogue identifies a document, not a byte-level version of the file behind the pathname. The local `netinfo/blacker.doc` is the later March 21, 1989 text at that same pathname, so the pathname was reused across editions and no catalogue entry dates which text sat there on a given day. KI-111 appears in none of the six editions.

#### DDNCOG minutes and decision-record leads

**The minutes of the August 1985 meeting are not held, and the search for them is now bounded.** `NETINFO:DDNCOG-MIN-1.TXT` was the live retrieval handle carried into this pass. It is genuinely absent from the local collections: a filename and content search over the 57 files of the netinfo archive and the 279 files of `etext/DDN`, each verified readable one by one, returns the newsletter and nothing else. This supplies a dated content-search result; the later dataless observation does not by itself establish what an earlier pass successfully read. The netinfo archive is also identified: 56 of its 57 files are byte-identical to the NIC netinfo directory on the *InternetInfo* CD-ROM, and the 57th is that CD's saved directory index. Its contents are a 1991-94 snapshot, so it is not a complete inventory of files offered in 1985 — a bounded negative with a stated reason, not evidence that the file never existed. Against that, the document is independently catalogued. CHM's [*DDN Library Index by Date*](https://archive.computerhistory.org/resources/access/text/2012/11/500001011-05-01-acc.pdf), compiled by Elizabeth Feinler in 2012, lists **85-1150**, "Minutes – First meeting of the Defense Data Network communications operations group," with the companion viewgraph set at **85-2099** and a later set of DDNCOG minutes at **89-3351**, "DCA Memo B650." That office code matches DDN News 43, which says the group was "chaired by the Defense Communications Agency (DCA) Code B645, Operations Management Division, (now Code B650)." Two catalogue readings conflict with the newsletter and are left unreconciled: the newsletter places the first meeting at **Arlington**, Virginia while 85-2099 says Vienna, and it announces the second meeting for **25-26 February 1986** while 86-2906 describes the second DCA group meeting as May 20-21, 1986. An announcement is not a record that a meeting took place. The index is a catalogue of a physical library, its own head note warns that the sort discarded second and third title lines, and inquiries remain outside this work, so 85-1150 is a better retrieval handle than the NIC filename rather than a recovered text. The **Internet Archive full-text search** that the September 14 session could not run was completed on September 16 and found nothing: 45 queries covering DDNCOG, its OCR variants, the filename forms and the group's name as a phrase. It completes that bounded search without recovering a copy, with two important limits. The tested queries failed to retrieve a known NIC pathname; `NETINFO:BLACKER.DOC` is in the OCR text of AD-A173060 and no query form returns it, so a mention only by pathname could have been missed by those queries. Queries using phrases from DDN News 43 likewise returned nothing. These failed controls limit the search; they do not establish a categorical exclusion of all NIC files or newsletters from the index. The September 16 pass also reported a DNS failure for `ia-fts.archive.org`; this later observation does not establish the cause of the September 14 proxy failure.

Read in full, the newsletter's account of that meeting adds context at the level of objectives rather than of the interface rule. Alongside the draft documents on display, "Configuration management of the network was discussed, and an overview of the accepted security architecture was presented," and "Representatives from the classified network segments discussed progress they have made using the secure architecture and DoD protocols. Other concerns about integration of the classified and unclassified segments of the network were also voiced." Those integration concerns, voiced in August 1985, sit on the same line as the IG's finding five years later that the classified network still had to be isolated. They bear on the fate of the consolidation objective that DDN inherited from AUTODIN II. They establish nothing about the addressing clause.

#### Continuity, approval, and limits on ancestry

**The sources support broader continuity without establishing interface ancestry.** The November 1983 guide cites DCA's *Technical Assessment of the Application of BLACKER to the Integrated AUTODIN System*, **April 12, 1983**, as a study to complete BLACKER requirements for DDN (printed **2, 29** / PDF **12, 37**, checked visually). [DDN News 41](https://ftp.ripe.net/rfc/museum/ddn-news/ddn-news.n41.1), October 18, 1985, places BLACKER in IASA's message-service architecture alongside DDN, I-S/A AMPE and AUTODIN subscriber terminals. That use of AUTODIN should not be equated with the cancelled AUTODIN II packet network. Separately, the [1985 Defense Science Board report](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA162888) compares BLACKER's accreditation difficulties with AUTODIN II's (printed **11** / PDF **25**) and explicitly connects DDN's consolidation and multilevel-security goals to those that motivated AUTODIN II (printed **12** / PDF **26**). Both pages were checked visually. This supports continuity of objectives, while leaving inheritance of the KI-111 address rule unestablished.

The decision sources are now better identified, though still unheld. The 1983 guide's references name DCA, Latham and NSA security-architecture papers from 1982 (printed **29–30** / PDF **37–38**), and the handbook's own *Guidance and Program Direction Applicable to the Defense Data Network and Other DoD Data Networks*, printed **1-47** / PDF **56**, independently confirms three of them by office, subject and date: a DUSD (C3I) memorandum, "Defense Data Network -- Security Architecture Options," **10 May 1982**; a Director of DCA memorandum of the same subject, **19 Nov 1982**; and a Director of NSA memorandum, "Defense Data Network," **1 Nov 1982**. The latter two are jointly called "the DDN Security Architecture Reports," and the facing page identifies the architecture they produced as "Option 2.2 -- WITH." Both pages were checked visually. The DSB report describes architecture reviews and working groups in 1984, but its narrative and appended agendas differ: the narrative dates a July review to the 10th and an unavailable October briefing to the 5th, while the agendas place the architecture sessions on July 11 and October 12 (printed **12–13, 23–25** / PDF **26–27, 34–36**, all checked visually). These dates have not been reconciled, and an agenda does not establish that a briefing occurred.

The records route has changed. The handbook's Configuration Control Group description (printed **1-24** / PDF **33**) is a less direct records lead: the CCG's stated remit is trouble reports, incident reports, software patches and network configuration changes, and the BLACKER programme's manager was DIRNSA. The handbook's overview section, printed **1-14 to 1-15** / PDF **23–24**, names a better-matched body — a Configuration Control Office in the Defense Communications System Directorate whose functions include documenting specifications, holding configuration control of specifications, and approving or disapproving proposed specifications — alongside the PSSG and PSTP. That is the same Executive Agent machinery Chunk 2 needs. **No dedicated series was identified by the September 14 schedule search.** That does not close the shared route: the general publications and ARPANET series described in [record schedules and SRI proposals](tcp-versions-and-standardization.md#dca-record-schedules-and-sri-contract-proposals) remain untested possibilities. A match of remit is not evidence that the BFE ICD passed through this office, and a failure to find its name in schedules is not evidence that its records do not survive.

The **February 2, 1987** approval is no longer a tool-summary lead. [DoD IG report 90-097](https://web.archive.org/web/20251207235621/https://media.defense.gov/1990/Jul/05/2001714385/-1/-1/1/90-097.pdf), *Defense Data Network*, July 5, 1990, 99 PDF pages, states at printed **2** / PDF **11**, checked visually: "On February 2, 1987, the ASD(C3I) approved the Defense Data Network Security Architecture (the Architecture), the framework for the security of the Network, which revised the security plan to protect communications that was specified in the original Program Plan." Recommendation 3.g at printed **55** / PDF **64** and the benefits table at printed **84** / PDF **93** both name the instrument a **memorandum**. It approved a revision, not a first architecture: the December 1985 handbook already prints an "approved" architecture, and DDN News 43 reports an "accepted security architecture" presented in August 1985. The audit does not otherwise bear on BLACKER. Its encryption-deployment findings concern **KG-84A** link encryptors on inter-switch trunks and host access lines (printed **29** / PDF **38**); KI-111, BFE and TSEC appear nowhere in the report, and BLACKER appears once, in the appended Army memorandum quoted above. In 1990 the DCA was still "continuing with our study on host encryption alternatives," and a revised architecture had been drafted and put on hold (printed **55** / PDF **64**). Separately, at printed **28** / PDF **37** the report records that "the classified Network currently must be isolated from unclassified circuits," which "conflicts with one of the original goals of the Network, which was to have a fully integrated data communications system in DoD." That bears on the fate of the consolidation objective, not on the address rule.

#### Guide searches and related technical accounts

**The guide was not recovered from the searched routes; a December 1985 gateway report describes a BLACKER address binding.** The September 16 search for the guide as a document ran the three routes the earlier handoff named. The Internet Archive metadata/full-text and NTIS title queries returned no copy, though the NTIS control returned the 1983 guide; and [Shirey's April 1990 *Computer Communication Review* paper](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/378570.378710), read in full, does not cite the guide and gives no MITRE report number for it (reference list at printed **71** / PDF **6**, checked visually). The search did turn up SPARTA's [*Department of Defense Gateway Architecture and Functional Requirements*](https://archive.org/details/DTIC_ADA169086), **11 December 1985** (AD-A169086). Its reference list at printed **56** / PDF **63**, checked visually, cites the companion *Evolution of Security Services* draft of 6 August 1985, marked "For Official Use Only. Distribution Limited to DoD and DoD Contractors Only," and does not cite the Subscriber Guide. That is evidence of a contemporary distribution restriction on the companion document, not the guide. The guide's marking, later release history, and the cause of its non-recovery remain unknown. Section 7.3 of the same report, at printed **54** / PDF **61**, checked visually, calls BLACKER "transparent to subscriber hosts (e.g., gateways)" and then says: "The use of BLACKER requires a red host to be bound with a black network host address." Section 7.2 plans BLACKER devices "between all network hosts, including gateways, and network packet switches." This is a third party writing three months before BBN's draft, and it is closer to BBN's reported address rule than anything in the recovered 1985 ICD text. It still states no source-and-destination matching, no connection keying and no restriction on common-user gateways, and it cites no source for the sentence. It shows that contemporaries understood an address binding to be part of BLACKER's design. It does not supply the p. 57 requirement.

Shirey's 1990 paper describes the architecture the guide concerned, by the same author, and expressly "does not offer a rationale for the features." Its Figure 1, at printed **67** / PDF **2**, checked visually, gives the same service taxonomy CSC attributed to the guide. For subcommunities of hosts and individual hosts, the figure says that DDN enforces trusted and closed subcommunities and that "Subscribers Use DDN Network Layer Address to Enforce Finer Granularity." That concerns discretionary access control, not BLACKER keying. Section 3.2, at printed **69** / PDF **4**, checked visually, places a BFE "on each host-to-switch access path of all hosts used by subscribers." The same section says a BFE encrypts the application data in each X.25 packet and leaves the header unencrypted. The paper names no KI-111 and states no address rule for gateways. It also says an earlier version was released for MILCOM '87 "too late for the conference proceedings," while three bibliographies cite Mundy and Shirey in the MILCOM '87 proceedings. That conflict is unreconciled. Separately, the SPARTA report's survey section, at printed **18** / PDF **25**, checked visually, describes the Ford Aerospace multinet gateway attaching to SACDIN "using the ADCCP Mode VI [DIN IIa] and Segment Interface Protocol developed for AUTODIN II [DIN IIb]." That is continuity at the protocol level in a 1985 multilevel gateway design. It is not ancestry of the address rule.

**The two remaining leads are closed, and neither held the guide.** On September 16 the two small leads left by the guide search were opened. NTIS **PB92119445**, the CALS DoD/Industry Steering Group's *Program Acquisition Manager's Guidelines for Implementing CALS Communications*, dated **April 1, 1989**, cites the companion *Evolution of Security Services: 1986-1992*, not the guide: at printed **55** / PDF **59** and in its references at printed **63** / PDF **67**, both checked visually. The section presents itself, at printed **50** / PDF **54**, as a summary of Gross and Wilder's *DoD OSI Implementation Strategy*, MITRE MTR 87W00155, 19 May 1988, which was not read.
