## Findings and limits

### The replacement DDN adopted a closer shared foundation with research networking

**Assessment.** Supported by the architecture and host-interface choices; distinguish the DDN backbone from the larger Internet.

**Evidence.** DDN Program Plan, January 1982, revised May 1982, pp. 1-3, 16-17, 157; BBN 6223, pp. 18-19, 24-25.

### The local DDN Program Plan is the ARPANET/WIN design team's own competing plan, not a post-decision plan for a chosen system

**Assessment.** Directly established. Its p. 1 still says the design "will be compared by DCA with an alternative approach" and that "this is not a proposal"; the April directive names the January 1982 ARPAnet Replica Program Plan; DCEC independently identifies a Replica team and a DIN II team.

**Evidence.** Program Plan, printed pp. 1, 257, and foreword p. iii, inspected visually; directive p. v; DCEC-TN-8-83, p. 1.

### The Program Plan's performance and cost figures compare the two competing designs

**Assessment.** Contradicted. Its figures are compared with the AUTODIN II A-Specification's requirements and with existing dedicated systems; the copy never costs or models the rival design. The unabridged May 1982 plan may have: DoD IG audit 90-097 says the Program Plan "included a comprehensive cost comparison of alternative program plans," though the phrase is undefined and the same report uses it for the dedicated-circuit comparison.

**Evidence.** Table 1.2-1, printed pp. 6-7; printed p. 7; printed pp. 254-255; IG 90-097 printed pp. 12, 14, 15, at [IG audit](ddn-selection.md#the-1990-inspector-general-audit).

### The 1981-82 selection was decided by a demonstrated performance advantage for the winning design

**Assessment.** Not established. Both proposed backbones were checked with shared analytical tools. DCEC reports insufficient delay criteria through January 1982, with a highest-priority constraint but none agreed for the remaining traffic; average packet sizes also differed. Its explicit warning of subsequent process changes prevents carrying that account forward as proof of the February evaluation's criteria. These are model outputs, not comparative operating measurements.

**Evidence.** DCEC-TN-8-83, executive summary iii / PDF p. 6; printed pp. 25, 26, and 30 / PDF pp. 34, 35, and 39.

### Cost was decisive in the selection

**Assessment.** Not established, but a comparative cost analysis existed and favored the winner. The evaluation team "adjusted and normalized for comparsion" both teams' estimates and "These cost estimates gave a heavy edge to REPLICA." Confidence was undermined by prime-contractor inputs to both teams under no obligation to perform as promised, and near-term termination costs ran against the change. The earlier reading of "no really convincing set of comparative costs" was a misattribution: that sentence concerns common user networks against dedicated links. The magnitude is now known: $588 million against $429 million in constant FY82 dollars, relayed from the February 23 draft sheet, and a $159 million "cost advantage" stated independently in a 1983 Air Force answer to the Senate. The Department's public rationale named cost with survivability, which shows that cost was a stated reason, not that it was decisive. DCA and ASD(C3I) conceded in 1990 that the May 1982 plan's $421 million total for FY 1982-92 was "soon recognized" as wrong, which bears on the absolute figures, not on the comparison.

**Evidence.** DSB ADA127476, printed pp. 21, 22, and 23, verified; ADA128127 printed p. 23; S. Hrg. 98-361 Pt. 2 p. 840; DoD Annual Report FY1984 p. 255; IG 90-097 printed pp. 8, 41; see [relayed evaluation](ddn-selection.md#the-relayed-evaluation-and-official-public-rationale).

**Assessment.** Incomplete as stated. The report also considers survivability, security, cost, user satisfaction, and development of a common-user network separately. The chart is not an exhaustive selection framework or the missing DCA evaluation's weighting.

**Evidence.** DSB ADA127476, printed p. 12 / PDF p. 20 and subsequent sections.

**Assessment.** Unsafe to assume. **Evidence.** DSB ADA127476, printed p. 12 / PDF p. 20.

**Assessment.** Contradicted. The shared traffic model computed TCP overhead in the forward direction and TCP acknowledgements in the reverse, with piggybacking, for each alternative design; "TCP/IP" is one of the modeling note's own key words.

**Assessment.** Partly supported at the level of network architecture: it discusses interoperability, interconnection, routing, and host interfacing. **Assessment.** Described second hand, with exact criteria and weights unavailable. ### Assessment of the selection evidence

There is strong evidence that AUTODIN II materially shaped TCP development, implementations, and the Defense Department's adoption of TCP/IP. There is also direct evidence that its particular protocol commitments diverged from the developing research protocols. The 1982 DDN plan proposes a reorientation toward ARPANET-derived infrastructure and standard TCP/IP while accommodating inherited requirements, and the April 2, 1982 directive adopts the plan it belongs to. This gives the proposed argument a contemporaneous mechanism.

The plan is the winning design team's own pre-decision submission: it states what that team proposed, not what was evaluated or built. The decision-side accounts in [DSB report](ddn-selection.md#the-defense-science-board-task-force-report) and [DCEC modeling account](ddn-selection.md#dcecs-fly-off-modeling-account), verified against rendered pages, establish three distinctions.

First, the sequence of recommendations and authorization is established. **The DCA evaluation panel concluded on February 25, 1982 and the Director of DCA approved the next day.** The Defense Science Board Task Force reviewed the ongoing evaluation concurrently and submitted its concurring recommendations on March 8, followed by briefings on March 9 and 12. **Carlucci's April 2 directive made the departmental termination and replacement decision.** The Task Force report describes the DCA evaluation from outside it; that evaluation's own record remains missing.

Second, comparisons were made. The evaluation team normalized both design teams' cost estimates and they "gave a heavy edge to REPLICA"; comparative survivability analysis produced drawdown curves; and both proposed backbones were checked with shared analytical tools. These are estimates and model calculations, not measurements of the two completed networks. DCEC reports inadequate performance criteria through January 1982, including a defined delay constraint for the highest-priority packets but no agreed delay criterion for the remaining traffic. Its account expressly warns that the process would have changed before publication, so it cannot settle what criteria were used in February. The Task Force separately questions the firmness of contractor cost commitments.

Third, the protocol-name search carries no explanatory weight. No TCP or IP name was found in the Task Force report's extracted text, but the report argues from interoperability, interconnection of existing nets, routing strength, and three levels of host interfacing. DCEC's modeling note computed TCP overhead and TCP acknowledgements for both designs. **The evidence does not support treating architectural convergence solely as a consequence of the selection.** Protocol structure entered the engineering comparison, and internetwork architecture entered the recommendation's reasoning. Their weight in the final choice remains unestablished.

A defensible working thesis is: **TCP/IP developed amid overlapping operational and research projects whose requirements did not always agree. AUTODIN II was a major intended destination, but its protocol commitments had to be reconciled with an evolving internetwork architecture. The shift to DDN brought the operational defense network onto a foundation more closely shared with research networking, while leaving some of those tensions unresolved.**

The interaction ran in both directions: internetting research informed AUTODIN II work, and AUTODIN II requirements influenced protocol development. A separate AUTODIN II branch is evidence of this relationship and its coordination problems. It does not by itself undermine the argument. The strongest formulation identifies which requirements diverged, where they were implemented, and how compatibility was sought. Exclusive original purpose or a single cause for every subsequent architectural choice is unnecessary to that account.

DDN's closer relationship to the emerging Internet is supported at the level of shared packet-switching technology, host TCP/IP, and the use of gateways to attach local networks. The 1982 plan still describes DDN I as a single integrated packet-switching backbone. Keep that backbone distinct from the larger internetwork in which it participated. BBN's 1986 report explicitly discusses both the larger Internet and local networks connecting to DDN while maintaining autonomy.

### Cancellation and replacement

*DoD Network Newsletter*, ANEWS-12, June 11, 1982, opening section.

[DoD Network Newsletter 12](https://ftp.ripe.net/rfc/museum/ddn-news/ddn-news.n12.1)

DCA explains its reorganization as implementing the decision to cancel AUTODIN II in favor of the ARPANET-based Defense Data Network. The DDN Program Plan examined in the follow-up review supplies the architecture behind this notice: a different switching infrastructure, preferred host TCP/IP implementations, and accommodations for older subscriber interfaces. The relevant change occurred within defense networking and brought it closer to the architecture used by the research Internet. The detailed evidence is in the next section.

#### Design and technology considerations

**What the Task Force said favored the ARPANET/WIN approach.** "As a result of the evaluation, the Task Force found that design and technology considerations strongly favored the ARPANET/WIN approach as a basis for the development of a computer communications network," and "It found these advantages to be more substantial than the DCA evaluation suggested that they were" (**p. 12**). Its chart lists **six design-and-technology considerations**: maturity of technology, state-of-the-art, evolutionary potential, security approach, manpower, and interconnection issues (**p. 12**). These do not exhaust the evaluation: the same page separately names survivability, security, cost, user satisfaction, and development of a common-user network.

- **Maturity.** "The operational use of ARPANET, WIN, COINS, PLATFORM has given the defense community many years experience with the technology involved," and "the extensive upgrading of the ARPANET software over the years is another important feature of system maturity" (**p. 12**).
- **State of the art.** This maturity "came without the costs of outmoded technology": the BBN C-30 "is a step ahead of the PDP-11 used in AUTODIN II," the REPLICA topology is "substantially more sophisticated," and "the routing algorithm used in the ARPANET system is substantially stronger than that developed for AUTODIN II" (**p. 13**). This is a technical comparison of network architecture, and the first pass omitted it entirely.
- **Evolutionary potential.** "substantially greater than that of AUTODIN II" (**p. 13**), argued from government ownership against a leased system and from "a large experimental test bed" in the ARPANET that carries "significant throughput of material by a large number of users employing a wide range of user operations."
- **Security.** Difficulties with the security kernel approach "proved to be substantial," turning on verifying the integrity of the software, recertification after each software change, and the denial of internal measurement while debugging (**p. 13**). End-to-end encryption "tends to decouple security from almost all other issues," while "the achievement of true multi-level security depends on the availability of both hardware and trusted software not yet available" (**p. 14**). Operationally, the AUTODIN II kernel-based switch carried "some 150,000 lines of source code as compared with 15,000 lines of object code in the ARPANET switch" (**p. 20**). The quoted counts use different units, source code and object code; they do not establish a tenfold size ratio. The report also judges secure operation possible for either design (**p. 18**) and both adequately protected with NSA's proposed modifications (**p. 20**). Its preference concerns maintenance and certification costs, not a finding that AUTODIN II could never be secure. IPLI is "an interim solution" and BLACKER is to be given higher priority (**pp. 1, 24-25, 27**).
- **Manpower.** REPLICA gains "a clear advantage by having unmanned switches located in secure facilities where part-time maintenance is available when required" (**p. 14**).
- **Interconnection.** "A number of interconnection issues favor the ARPANET/WIN approach as well." Incremental growth is "clearly more compatible with the ARPANET design," and "there is a standing set of networks already existing which can be pulled together to form ultimately a true common user network operating at all security levels" (**p. 14**). ARPANET's "extensive host interface development" amplifies that advantage (**p. 15**).
- **Certification as an institutional problem.** "NSA is in the position of rendering security judgments without having functional responsibilities for the working of the network" (**p. 19**), which makes it "extraordinarily difficult for the operational authority to make decisions to use a system in the face of NSA concerns about security risks." The Task Force's position was that "the most expeditious approach is probably to accept NSA's recommendation for improving the security architecture of whichever system is chosen; but to work in the future toward a more balanced assessment of security requirements" (**p. 20**).

#### Comparative cost and survivability evidence

**Which comparisons were actually made.** Verification corrected the first pass in three places, all in the same direction: more was compared than the earlier summary allowed.

- **Survivability was compared, and produced a modeled result.** "The DCA evaluation of comparative survivability was made within a narrow range of considerations focusing on a measure of the graceful degradation of the two systems under varying levels of attack. Because it has more nodes, the ARPANET approach obviously fared better in this analysis" (**p. 17**). "In the case of ARPANET, an optimized nuclear attack on 20 nodes would reduce system connectivity to about ten percent. Comparable effects occurred in the AUTODIN II system with the loss of eight nodes" (**p. 17**). Two drawdown curves are reproduced at **pp. 17-18**. The Task Force attributes these results to DCA's evaluation. DCEC describes a related drawdown method in [DCEC modeling account](ddn-selection.md#dcecs-fly-off-modeling-account); the attribution and general method are documented, but the inputs and runs needed to reproduce these exact curves are not held. What the Task Force called ill-defined was the criteria, not the analysis: survivability considerations "are ill-defined but seem to favor WIN/ARPANET," with "lack of agreed survivability criteria" making "objective appraisal difficult" (**p. 15**), and Soviet weapons inventory growth making the cost of attacking either system "almost trivial in terms of the amount of node proliferation that is included in either design" (**p. 16**).
- **Costs were compared, and the comparison favored the winner.** "Total estimated design team costs were then adjusted and normalized for comparsion by the evaluation team. **These cost estimates gave a heavy edge to REPLICA**" (**p. 21**; the misspelling is in the original). The Task Force doubted their absolute validity because "there was so heavy a promissory ingredient in the proposal costs," the principal factor being that "in both proposals significant inputs had been made by prime contractor supporting the design teams" under no contractual obligation to perform as promised (**p. 21**). Its conclusion was that costs "were uncertain, to say the least, but would probably favor the WIN/ARPANET approach" (**p. 21**). Near-term costs ran the other way, because of "the fairly substantial termination costs associated with ending the Western Union leasing arrangements," against a judgment that keeping AUTODIN II would "incur significantly higher total costs in the long run" (**p. 22**).
- **The "no convincing comparative costs" sentence is about something else.** At **p. 23** the report says "No really convincing set of comparative costs for the common user networks and dedicated links was at hand." That concerns common-user service against dedicated links, not REPLICA against AUTODIN II. The first pass used it for the latter; **withdraw that use.**

#### Evaluation procedure and contrary evidence

**The evaluation procedure, described from outside it.** "The use of two design teams to prepare what were essentially proposals served to force a binary decision in the evaluation. Thus, the selection was made between one of the two approaches rather than an attempt to find a solution to the problem which was in some sense optimized" (**p. 7**; the first pass misquoted this as "to produce essentially proposals" and placed it at pp. 7-8). The chart on the same page describes the evaluation process as a separate team using "Well established criteria emulating contractor selection" and a "Complex quantitative method based on disaggregated subjective judgments," with "Care given to avoidance of bias." In prose: "The evaluation team's determination was undertaken on the basis of a complex quantitative method which relied in the end on highly disaggregated judgments. There is little else that could have been done" (**p. 9**). The evaluation's recorded conclusion is one line: **"REPLICA Approach Provides the Best (SIC) DoD Data Network"** (**p. 9**; the "(SIC)" is printed in the report). The Task Force's appraisal of that evaluation lists: reached without regard to AUTODIN II difficulties; noted nearly all of the WIN/ARPANET technology advantages the Task Force itself identified; perceptively noted contractor promises without commitments, the shortcomings of an either/or decision, and risks; conscious of the importance of user satisfaction; some uneven treatment; carefully unbiased (**p. 9**, expanded at **pp. 10-11**). The risks the evaluation noted include the rapid production of C-30 switches, "the capacity of BB&N as a relatively small firm to fulfill the prime contract responsibilities," Western Union's management performance, and URDB inadequacies (**p. 10**). Survivability treatment in the evaluation was "fairly good though limited," and security "appeared to be given limited consideration" (**p. 11**). **All of this is second hand; the evaluation team's own report is still not held.**

**Contrary evidence printed inside the report.** John Stenbit's note appears in the body at **p. 12**: the Task Force Evaluation "tends to focus on considerationns it felt supported the choice of the ARPANET approach, and consequently masks a substantially less obvious choice between the two systems," and while he "does not take issue with the choice made," he believes it "has greater utility as guidance to proceeding with the implementation of an ARPANET decision than in providing the basis for making such a decision." Preserve this. A named member of the recommending body recorded that the report reads as justification more than as decision basis, and the first pass missed it.

#### Recommendations and architectural reasoning

**The recommendations, p. 27**, "made to USDR&E on March 12, 1982": reaffirm the need for a common-user network; "Make a choice between AUTODIN II and the WIN/ARPANET approach promptly; our review favors the selection of WIN/ARPANET"; "In pursuing the development of a DDN, follow a program of gradual expansion of the WIN/ARPANET-compatible base with an eye toward the ultimate interconnection of individual nets"; and develop new security safeguard devices like BLACKER. The preferred route to a common-user network is stated at **p. 23**: "the creation of discrete nets of users, accommodating user-community peculiarities, but maintaining a compatibility between nets that would allow for their future consolidation. This is a bottom-up rather than a top-down approach."

**Protocol-name search and positive architectural evidence.** A search of the page-delimited text finds no occurrence of TCP, IP as a standalone token, Transmission Control Protocol, Internet Protocol, X.25, or "layer" in that extraction. A fresh extraction repeats the result but uses the same OCR layer, so it is not independent evidence that the scan contains no occurrence. Positive passages make the search uninformative about architectural reasoning. "Internet" does occur once, at **p. 3**, in "the consideration of alternative approaches by DoD Data Internet Study in 1975," which is a positive source lead; the first-pass record's stated negative covered protocol names, not the word "Internet" alone. "Protocols" occurs once, at **p. 39**, among the topics the follow-on DDN Task Force is told to address. And the report argues about network architecture throughout in its own vocabulary: interoperability (**pp. 3, 20, 25**), the JCS count of "as many as 58 separate systems requiring a capacity for interoperability" (**p. 3**), routing and topology (**p. 13**), interconnection of existing nets (**pp. 14, 23, 27**), the IPLI as a device that "serves as a gateway between nets" (**p. 24**), and host interfacing at **p. 25**, where "The WIN/ARPANET approach provides for three levels of host interfacing," the most desirable being "to modify host software so as to allow it to use the broad range of functions that are embodied in the ARPANET system," while "Other approaches will solve the problem but tend to become expensive in terms of true interoperability." That is the same three-option structure the Program Plan states in protocol terms at its printed p. 16. **The report explicitly reasons about internetwork architecture; the text search does not establish how particular protocols affected the selection.**

**Epilogue, pp. 28-31**, read in full in this pass and legible, contrary to the first pass's report of a degraded, fragment-only section. The DDN Program has moved "along the path outlined in the REPLICA Program Plan"; the Program Management Office holds 44 of its required positions; WIN IMP hardware is ordered; "The reconfiguration of the REPLICA into a MILNET for operational users and an experimental network is in the final planning stages with a logical separation of the network planning for Mid-1983"; MINET's final IMP is due November 1983; SACDIN IMP and Monitoring Center hardware is ordered and "SIP interface development has been initiated"; and access control for dialing subscribers, "a capability which was not provided for in the REPLICA Program Plan," is under contract (**p. 28**). A subscriber interface guide is due November 1982 and a final interface specification by January 1983 (**p. 29**). The Task Force reviewed progress on October 6-7, 1982. The Program Office's favored security architecture, "Option 2.2," is "separate classified and unclassified networks to be connected ultimately through switch-level gates allowing classified traffic to exploit routing through the unclassified network but denying unclassified users access to the classified network," which the Task Force supported; NSA raised questions "about connecting MINET in Europe with MILNET in the U.S." (**p. 29**).

**Background the report supplies for other chunks, recorded and not pursued.** Printed **p. 3** says AUTODIN II "was undertaken in 1976 after the consideration of alternative approaches by DoD Data Internet Study in 1975," and that "In July 1980, an OSD review group was established to review the system. With the assistance of DCA, the group considered the cost, security, performance, and survivability of AUTODIN II" and "explored available options if AUTODIN II failed." The [page image](https://archive.org/download/DTIC_ADA127476/DTIC_ADA127476.pdf#page=11) clearly reads **1980**; **1960** is an OCR error retained in the raw extraction. Both the 1975 study and the 1980 review group are retrieval targets. The review concerns the whole program; whether it explains the TCP-specific upgrade or deferral requires evidence from [Chunk 2](tcp-versions-and-standardization.md#research-question). Printed **p. 4** adds that "In August 1981, DCA agreed to conduct a formal, comparative evaluation of AUTODIN II and its alternative," which sits one month before the September 1981 direction DCEC records. One further discrepancy is preserved rather than reconciled: p. 3 dates AUTODIN II's start to 1976, while Appendix A at p. 33 says "under development since 1977 as a leased service from Western Union."

### DCEC’s fly-off modeling account

R. Prishivalko, *Modeling Efforts for the AUTODIN II "Fly-Off" Study*, Defense Communications Engineering Center, Systems Analysis Division R820, 1860 Wiehle Avenue, Reston VA. **Technical Note No. 8-83, August 1983**, 38 pp., approved for public release, [DTIC ADA134275](https://archive.org/download/DTIC_ADA134275/DTIC_ADA134275.pdf).

**Verified September 12, 2026.** The Internet Archive mirror of the DTIC file matches the fingerprint in the [verification record](../sources/records/ddn-reports-page-verification.json). That record gives bibliographic cross-checks, text-layer coverage, and the exact pages inspected visually in the verification and subsequent independent validation. Printed page = PDF page minus 9 through the numbered body. The executive summary is printed iii / PDF p. 6. Text-layer-only passages are distinguished by the recorded coverage.

**Two limits the document sets on itself, both new.** Its foreword says DCEC Technical Notes "are intended to stimulate thinking and encourage information exchange; but they do not represent an approved position or policy of DCEC, and should not be used as authoritative guidance for related planning and/or further action." Its executive summary says it "describes the status of the design process during the 'fly-off' period (i.e., through January 1982)." The executive summary also warns that significant changes in the process would have occurred before publication. **It does not establish the criteria in force at the evaluation panel's February 25, 1982 conclusion or report that panel's final reasoning.** Within those limits it remains the closest examined account to the missing evaluation record, because DCEC supported **both** design teams and the evaluation team.

- **The arrangement.** "In September 1981, the Director, DCA, General Hilsman, directed that a study be done to reassess the direction of the AUTODIN II planning, which would culminate in a Defense Data Network (DDN)." The design teams were "the DIN II team, which extended previous AUTODIN II plans in consonance with Western Union, and the Replica Team, which proceeded from technology and concepts previously applied in the ARPANET and WIN"; "a third team was selected to evaluate the two alternatives once they were specified" (**p. 1**). DCEC "collected and refined basic network requirements; modified, explained, and applied computer models used in the alternative network designs; verified the proposed designs by use of computer models; and evaluated the network survivability offered by the two DDN alternatives" (**p. 1**). This independently dates the tasking to September 1981, matching the Program Plan's foreword and the Task Force's terms of reference, and it names the losing plan's provenance.
- **A shared and defective requirements baseline.** "An ADP system as described in the URDB might not correspond with the real system" (**p. 2**). "Frequently, the requested line speed cannot accommodate the projected traffic"; location names are "invalid or misspelled"; the mode field carries "suspicious entries." "The net result is that, even for the ADP systems represented therein, the URDB proved to be far from the usable collection of clean user requirements that any worthwhile design effort needs" (**p. 7**; the first pass placed this and the defect list at pp. 2-3). The reconstructed baseline is "91 ADP systems comprising 488 host computers, 87 remote concentrators, and 1359 terminals" (**p. 8**) -- the same 91 systems and 488 hosts the Program Plan uses in its section 1.2.1, with a different terminal count from the plan's 1446. Preserve that discrepancy.
- **The shared traffic model computed TCP overhead, for both designs.** This is new to the brief and it bears directly on the account. "Generally, the overhead specifications included consideration of segment overhead and packet overhead, both Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) overhead in the forward flow direction, and TCP acknowledgements (ACKS) in the reverse data flow direction. The practice of piggybacking TCP ACKS when possible was taken into account" (**p. 12**). The results are Table III, REPLICA Network Overhead (**p. 13**), and Table IV, AUTODIN II Overhead (**p. 14**), and "Each previously generated transaction trunk dataset was used with the appropriate overhead factors to generate, for each alternative design, the peculiar bit flows and packet flows that are required." The report's DD-1473 key words include "TCP/IP," and its acronym list expands TCP (**p. 31**). Protocol structure entered the engineering comparison as a quantity, although no TCP name was found in the advisory report's extracted text in [DSB report](ddn-selection.md#the-defense-science-board-task-force-report).
- **Shared tools.** GUPTA for switch-site selection (**p. 17**); CULINES for access-line costing, which "was used to provide a standard costing for both alternatives" (**p. 18**); MUXLOC/CONLOC for concentrator placement, with the caution that these "are heuristic approaches" that cannot "guarantee the optimum" (**p. 19**); and GRINDER for backbone design (**pp. 19-21**), "developed by Network Analysis Corporation (NAC) in the mid-1970's," described as "an analytic model, rather than a simulation," and "still apparently a state-of-the-art tool" whose "accuracy has been verified against actual ARPANET performance" (**p. 21**). That is a validation of the tool, not a measurement of either candidate system. Costing used common rate-step tables over the AT&T 260-series and DDS tariffs, maintained by the comptroller's office at DCA Headquarters (**pp. 21-22**).
- **An asymmetry in the tools, newly recorded.** The executive summary says "The network design process was hampered by attempting to rapidly apply existing design tools suitable for the AUTODIN II approach to the different Replica approach," and the conclusions warn that comparing two designs "requires extra caution in making assumptions and approximations. The concern is that neither design be affected more than the other" (**p. 30**). This documents difficulty adapting existing tools, not a finding that every tool originated with AUTODIN II or that the outcome was biased; GRINDER's reported ARPANET validation is a relevant qualification.
- **Both proposed backbones were checked through calculations.** "The backbone portion of each proposed network was cost and performance verified." Trunk costs were computed on the appropriate rate-step datasets, a switch-to-switch traffic matrix was built from the access-area homings with "both the data bits and the appropriate traffic-dependent overhead bits for the respective system," GRINDER routed the traffic and computed per-packet delays with worst cases noted, and tandem packet loads were checked "to verify that the number of IMP's/PSN's proposed for each site could handle the expected packet load" (**p. 25**).
- **Delay criteria were incomplete within the period this report covers.** The section heading at **p. 25** is "LACK OF PERFORMANCE CRITERIA." "An adequate set of performance criteria did not exist in the DIN II 'fly-off'. The only performance constraint clearly defined for both designs was that a Category I packet (i.e., highest priority, see System Performance Specification for AUTODIN II, Phase I), with an assumed size of 600 bits, be able to traverse the backbone between any switch pair in 200 milliseconds. No minimum delay was agreed to for the remaining 99% of the traffic, with the result that the numerous performance outputs of the modeling programs could not easily be used for evaluation purposes" (**p. 26**; the first pass gave pp. 25-26). The incommensurability is then stated numerically: "No meaningful conclusions can be drawn from the delay calculations for the average size packets of each network. The average packet size in one network was 996 bits, in the other 4499 bits. In other words, the information content of the 'average' packet differs greatly between the two networks, so a direct comparison of 'average' packet delays provides no immediate insight" (**p. 26**). The author calls for acceptable user-to-user delays for whole transactions, which would account for access links and segmentation as well as backbone packet delay. The concluding judgment, still bounded by the report's January cutoff, is: "Performance criteria were never specified to a degree sufficient to permit meaningful performance comparison of the two designs" (**p. 30**).
- **Survivability was compared; the results are withheld here.** "Comparison of the survivability of the two network backbones was another goal of the evaluation process" (**p. 27**). The measure is the number of terminal-to-host and host-to-host pairs that can still communicate as switch sites are removed, host-to-host weighted more heavily because "the two design teams had considered different sets important," evaluated worst-case by removing at each step the site that most diminishes the measure (**p. 27**). A nuclear variant assuming "all switch sites within a 6 mile radius of the targeted site would be destroyed along with the target" was added precisely "to produce drawdown curves for each of the two designed backbones that could more meaningfully be compared" (**p. 29**). Figure 2 at **p. 28** is a drawdown curve, but it is labelled a **sample**, and the report says plainly that "Specific results from the models are not presented herein" (**p. 30**). Correct the first pass here in both directions: a drawdown curve is in this document, and the comparative curves for the two designs are not -- those appear in [DSB report](ddn-selection.md#the-defense-science-board-task-force-report) at ADA127476 pp. 17-18, attributed there to the DCA evaluation.
- **The agreed criterion is also a Chunk 1 link.** The single performance criterion cites the *System Performance Specification for AUTODIN II, Phase I* -- the November 1975 document that [Chunk 1](priority-and-network-boundaries.md#the-recommendation-and-delivered-sequencing-endpoints) identified as its own missing link and that section [7](priority-and-network-boundaries.md#the-november-1975-system-performance-specification) tracks. It is also this document's only external citation: the technical note carries no reference list and names no evaluation-team report. This does not reopen Chunk 1; it adds one more citation to an unrecovered specification.

**What these two sources establish together.** The engineering comparison included TCP overhead, calculated delays, estimated costs, and modeled survivability. The Task Force explicitly considered network architecture, institutional arrangements, and security certification. Its six design-and-technology considerations sit alongside other factors, rather than supplying the whole selection framework. Its preference for the ARPANET approach coexists with recognition that either design could be secured and that termination brought near-term costs and service risks. DCEC reports inadequate delay criteria and difficulty adapting tools through January 1982, but explicitly warns of later changes; these limits cannot be projected unchanged into the February evaluation. The Task Force attributes favorable cost and survivability comparisons to DCA without supplying the full inputs or weighting. Stenbit's warning about the presentation, while supporting the choice, further limits its use as a complete account of deliberation. These sources establish substantive comparative reasoning, not a demonstrated operating advantage or the decisive weight of any single consideration.

### The relayed evaluation and official public rationale

Added by the second Chunk 4 pass on September 16, 2026. Coverage, fingerprints, page mappings and the full query log are in the [pass record](../sources/records/ddn-evaluation-and-public-rationale.json); the pages relied on are retained under [its pages directory](../sources/README.md#reading-and-retrieval-records). Every passage below was read as a page image. The evaluation team's report is still not held.

**A working paper of the evaluation is now identified by title and date.** An NPS master's thesis, Mary McLendon-Koenig, *Impact of the WIS Modernization Plan on the Joint Deployment System*, March 1983 ([DTIC ADA128127](https://archive.org/download/DTIC_ADA128127/DTIC_ADA128127.pdf)), lists as its reference 15 "Factor Comparison Narrative Summary Sheet, DRAFT, For Official Use Only, 23 February 1982, n. pag." (printed p. 77 / PDF p. 80, checked in both the DTIC copy and the NPS library scan, whose OCR misreads the month). The date is two days before the panel's recorded conclusion of February 25 at [DSB report](ddn-selection.md#the-defense-science-board-task-force-report). The thesis names no issuing office, so identifying the sheet as the evaluation team's rests on its title, date and content. Its contemporary For Official Use Only marking may help explain restricted circulation, but establishes neither current availability nor the cause of its absence from the searched catalogues.

**What the thesis relays from that sheet.** These are a student's paraphrases, each cited "[Ref. 15]", on printed pp. 22-24 / PDF pp. 25-27:

- Evaluation factors "were survivability, security, system design, and cost."
- The Replica "seemed better able to withstand network element losses, proposed a more flexible routing algorithm, and afforded a greater mobility capability."
- "In the area of large data transfers, AUTODIN II was superior to the Replica design," because the Replica's smaller packets "increase the overhead traffic through the system and can degrade system performance." This is consistent with DCEC's per-design TCP overhead tables at [DCEC modeling account](ddn-selection.md#dcecs-fly-off-modeling-account), though the thesis does not say the judgment came from them.
- "If AUTODIN II had been available for implementation during the evaluation effort time frame, the technology, schedule, and cost risks associated with the Replica proposal would certainly have cancelled some of the benefits. However, having no satisfactory AUTODIN II system online, the benefits of the Replica approach justified the risks."
- "In constant FY82 dollars, AUTODIN II total system cost was estimated at $588 million where the Replica total system cost was $429 million," with projected annual costs drawn as Figure 2.3.

The figure and its prose do not agree in detail. The text puts the Replica peak at $71 million around 1985, falling to "the $40 million range in 1987"; the curve peaks a little below $70 million and settles near $35 million. The text has AUTODIN II rising to $72 million "until 1995"; the curve peaks near $72 million about 1987 and settles near $55 million by about 1995. No weights or scores are relayed. The sentence that AUTODIN II "now had a six-year old design" against an eight-year computer life carries no citation and may be the author's own.

**The cost difference is corroborated by an independent witness.** An Air Force answer for the record in the Senate's FY 1984 defense appropriations hearings says: "The total cost (all appropriations) of DDN program is projected to be $535 million for FY 82-88 (escalated FY 82 dollars). The cost advantage of the DDN program over AUTODIN II is calculated to be $159 million in (unescalated) FY 82 dollars" (S. Hrg. 98-361, Pt. 2, printed p. 840, Internet Archive `micro_IA41152844_0773`). $588 million less $429 million is $159 million. Two separate documents therefore report the same difference, with an official Air Force statement in 1983. They may draw on the same underlying estimates. This corroborates a reported $159 million advantage, consistent with the "heavy edge to REPLICA" recorded at [DSB report](ddn-selection.md#the-defense-science-board-task-force-report). It does not establish that the evaluation team produced the figures rather than normalizing the design teams' own, and it does not answer the Task Force's doubt about their absolute validity. The same answer says that the FY 1983 President's Budget, submitted before "SECDEF direction to switch from AUTODIN II to the DDN program," requested $2 million for AUTODIN II and nothing for DDN.

**The contemporaneous public framing was survivability, then survivability and cost.** Weinberger's report to Congress of February 8, 1982, written during the evaluation, says: "As a consequence of our increased emphasis on system survivability and endurance, we are evaluating whether AUTODIN II remains the most attractive option for common-user data communications. Alternative options under consideration include a larger number of smaller communications nodes employed in networks such as the World Wide Military Command and Control System (WWMCCS) Intercomputer Network (WIN) and the ARPANET" ([DTIC ADA113991](https://archive.org/download/DTIC_ADA113991/DTIC_ADA113991.pdf), printed III-85 / PDF p. 166). The evaluation was to be finished before the first operational user moved to AUTODIN II, "now scheduled for March 1982." A year later the *Department of Defense Annual Report, Fiscal Year 1984* gives the whole public rationale in a clause: "we terminated the AUTODIN II program and replaced it with the more survivable and less costly DDN" (DTIC ADA160713, printed p. 255). Neither statement mentions architecture, maturity, security certification or protocols. They show which criteria the department chose to state publicly; they are not evidence of how the criteria were weighted inside the evaluation.

**A pre-evaluation sequence, at second hand.** Citing Stephen T. Walker, "Department of Defense Data Network," *Signal*, October 1982, the thesis says that IOC slipped repeatedly from 1979 until July 1980, when ASD(C3I) requested a review with alternatives; that in July 1981 DUSD(C3I) "questioned the wartime survivability of AUTODIN II," the doubt focusing on "a small number of switching nodes"; that "Immediately after this, the Air Force Test Director issued a report concerning the increasing cost of the system and doubts about the technology and future system performance"; and that DCA's third team was "a 30-day evaluation team" that "was to establish guidance for the two design teams and develop evaluation criteria" (printed p. 22). The July 1980 review matches the OSD review group at ADA127476 printed p. 3. The other two events are new and unheld. Treat the sequence with caution: the thesis says the Deputy Under Secretary ordered termination, when the order was Deputy Secretary Carlucci's, but the Walker pagination is not a contradiction: p. 42 is the article's starting page, p. 45 can be a pinpoint within it, and 41 is an index item number. **The starting-page citation is confirmed.** AFCEA's own *Index to Signal*, Volume 37, September 1982 through August 1983, printed in the October 1983 issue, lists it as title-index item 41, "Department of Defense Data Network," **Oct., p. 42** (printed p. 85), and its Author Index at printed p. 90, which states that its numbers are title-index item numbers, gives "Walker, Stephen T. 41"; both were read in the page images on September 17, 2026, at [DCA requirements and SIP identity](priority-and-network-boundaries.md#dca-requirements-sip-identity-and-the-classified-appendix). The Internet Archive holds the 1982-83 volume indexes but no issues, so the article's text is still unread and everything the record takes from it remains relayed through the thesis.

**The program's state before the alternatives.** An OSD C3I program sheet among the Carter-Reagan transition papers, late 1980, with Walker named as director, records three switching centers and the test facility installed, site testing complete "with deficiencies noted to be cleared," system testing "projected to begin in January following repeated delays," and "Projected IOC if tests are successfully completed is mid March 1981." Its Issues and "Decisions Jan - June 81" fields are blank (Folder 2, PDF p. 125). The accompanying plans list names a *Defense Audit Service Report on the Review of AUTODIN II* (No. 81-005), October 6, 1980 (PDF p. 115), a new retrieval handle.

**What this changes, and what it does not.** The thesis supplies a second-hand account of the evaluation, distinct from the Task Force's account. Its paraphrase names survivability, security, system design and cost as factors; relays prices for the alternatives; credits AUTODIN II with an advantage for large data transfers; and makes the choice conditional on AUTODIN II's unavailability. The Air Force's separately reported $159 million difference is consistent with those figures, although both may derive from the same estimates. The Task Force says its own preference was reached without regard to AUTODIN II programmatic deficiencies (executive summary, printed p. 1 / PDF p. 9). It also describes DCA's panel as deciding largely without relying on those difficulties, with a possible exception in the management overview that it says was not a major factor (printed p. 10 / PDF p. 18). Both passages were visually rechecked for private review. The thesis's availability-dependent account must remain distinct from both statements; the contrast cannot be resolved merely by assigning the accounts to different bodies. Recovering the comparison sheet could confirm, qualify or contradict it. The weighting, the scores and the final report remain missing, and everything from the sheet reaches us through the thesis's paraphrase.

### The 1990 Inspector General audit

Added by the third Chunk 4 pass on September 16, 2026. Coverage, the blank-page check, fingerprints and the page mapping are in the [pass record](../sources/records/ddn-audit-1990.json); the pages relied on are retained under [its pages directory](../sources/README.md#reading-and-retrieval-records). **The report does not discuss the selection.** It never names AUTODIN II. Its two uses of "AUTODIN" refer to the Automated Digital Network as an existing network in 1990 (printed pp. 44 and 51). It is a negative about this report, not evidence that the auditors lacked the records: they say they reviewed documents dated from October 1970.

**It independently dates the two 1982 plans.** "In April 1982, the Deputy Secretary of Defense directed the Director, Defense Communications Agency, to proceed with the development of the Network as outlined in the January 1982 ARPANET ... Replica Plan." That places a comparison after the panel's February 25 conclusion and before Carlucci's April 2 order. The concession comes from the department, not the auditors, and it is consistent with the Task Force's doubt about the absolute validity of the costs at [DSB report](ddn-selection.md#the-defense-science-board-task-force-report). Keep these figures apart from 9g's. The $421 million is development and operating cost for FY 1982-92; the relayed $429 million is the Replica's "total system cost" in constant FY82 dollars; the Air Force's $535 million and USD(R&E)'s $522.9 million both cover FY 1982-88 in escalated dollars. It also adds a same-year departmental admission that the plan's cost total was too low. The specific planned audit reading is complete. Other retrieval leads remain unheld; this does not establish exhaustion of public sources.
